# Novelty check and theory quotations: Pieces Without a Whole

Run 24 October 2026, after registration (prereg/PREREGISTRATION.md) and before any product was coded. Purpose: find prior work that already makes contributions C1 to C4, and collect verified quotations for the theory section.

Contributions tested:
- C1. Christensen's "verifiable" interface condition split into V1 syntactic, V2 provenance, V3 referent; learner-state interfaces fail V3 even where signing solves V2; this, not missing standards or missing translation, explains why ed-tech has not cohered into a learning operating system.
- C2. Market-wide test: 1EdTech certification counts by layer plus a coded classification of 40 to 60 products against a five-function operating-system test.
- C3. Audit of where AI products write learner evidence.
- C4. AI will not close the gap because the bottleneck is verification, not translation.

## How to read this file

- OPENED: the full text was opened by me or a research helper, as a local file, a curl copy, or a pdftotext extraction saved under study/research/novelty_scratch/. Every quotation in an OPENED entry was seen in that copy.
- DIGEST: seen only through a fetch tool's summary of a page; any quotation from it is marked UNVERIFIED.
- UNVERIFIED: not opened; cited for the record only.
- Closeness ratings: SAME CLAIM, PARTIAL, ADJACENT, or COUNTER-POSITION (a published view the paper argues against).
- Page numbers: "p." is a printed page seen in the copy; "pdf p." is a position in the saved file.
- Searches that returned a quota or rate-limit error are recorded as "source exhausted", never as "nothing found".
- Plain text, no em dashes; dashes inside quotations are reproduced as the source printed them only where unavoidable, otherwise replaced by a comma and marked [...] when text is cut.

## 1E. Theory precedents for "verifiable" as third-party checkable

Not in the brief's list; a strategy or economics referee will raise them. None is prior art for the ed-tech claim; all are the right citations for the V-level split.

**1E.1 Baldwin, Carliss Y. 2008. "Where do transactions come from? Modularity, transactions, and the boundaries of firms."** Industrial and Corporate Change 17 (1): 155-195. https://doi.org/10.1093/icc/dtm036 OPENED (copy at people.computing.clemson.edu/~johnmc/courses/cpsc950/baldwintransactions.pdf; journal pages from running heads).
- p. 155: "Placing a transaction in a particular location in turn requires work to define, count (or measure), and pay for the transacted objects. The costs of this work (labeled mundane transaction costs) are generally low at the thin crossing points of the task network, which correspond to module boundaries."
- p. 164: "In contract theory, if both parties agree on the definition of what is transferred ('this is indeed a satisfactory widget'), the transfer is called 'observable.' If third parties can be brought in and also agree ('anyone can see this is a satisfactory widget'), the transfer is 'verifiable.' These implicit costs of observing and verifying are mundane transaction costs under my definition."
- p. 165: "My definition of 'counting' also subsumes all measuring processes that are used to verify the quality of the transacted object."
- Relevance: Baldwin already joins modularity to the observable/verifiable distinction and locates transactions where objects can be defined and counted. The paper's V3 is the contract-theory "verifiable" (a third party can agree). Cite for the level split.

**1E.2 Hart, Oliver. 2016-12-08. "Incomplete Contracts and Control."** Nobel Prize Lecture. https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/hart-lecture.pdf OPENED (pdftotext). Also published in American Economic Review 107 (7), 2017 (journal pages not seen).
- pdf p. 14: "This would indeed be possible if c were verifiable, but at first sight seems impossible if c is merely observable."
- pdf p. 18: "the realization of c is then observable to both parties. However, c is not verifiable."
- Relevance: canonical statement that observable-but-not-verifiable terms push parties toward ownership and integration. Theory precedent only.

**1E.3 W3C. 2025-05-15. "Verifiable Credentials Data Model v2.0."** W3C Recommendation. https://www.w3.org/TR/vc-data-model-2.0/ OPENED (curl).
- Terminology, validation: "The assurance that a claim from a specific issuer satisfies the business requirements of a verifier for a particular use. This specification defines how verifiers verify verifiable credentials and verifiable presentations. It also specifies that verifiers validate claims in verifiable credentials before relying on them. However, the means for such validation vary widely and are outside the scope of this specification. Verifiers trust certain issuers for certain claims and apply their own rules to determine which claims in which credentials are suitable for use by their systems."
- Terminology, verifiable credential: "A tamper-evident credential whose authorship can be cryptographically verified."
- Trust model: "How verifiers decide which issuers to trust, and for what data or purposes, is out of scope for this recommendation."
- Also in my saved copy: "Verification of a credential does not imply evaluation of the truth of claims encoded in the credential." and "Verifiability of a credential does not imply the truth of claims encoded therein."
- Relevance: SAME DISTINCTION as the paper's V2 versus V3, already standardized. The spec's "verification" is V2; its "validation", declared out of scope, is V3. CLR 2.0 and Open Badges 3.0 are built on this model. The paper must cite this and state that its contribution is (a) making the V3 gap the explanatory variable for adoption across layers and (b) arguing education cannot close it without reassessment. Not prior art for the structural claim about ed-tech.

## 3D. Health informatics: interoperability levels, syntax before meaning (helper D; all OPENED by helper)

**T-9. HIMSS. "Definition of Interoperability, Approved by the HIMSS Board of Directors April 5, 2013."** One-page PDF; live URL 404; Wayback copy of https://www.himss.org/sites/hde/files/d7/FileDownloads/HIMSS%20Interoperability%20Definition%20FINAL.pdf (saved agentD/himss2013/). p. 1:
- "In healthcare, interoperability is the ability of different information technology systems and software applications to communicate, exchange data, and use the information that has been exchanged."
- "There are three levels of health information technology interoperability: 1) Foundational; 2) Structural; and 3) Semantic."
- "'Foundational' interoperability allows data exchange from one information technology system to be received by another and does not require the ability for the receiving information technology system to interpret the data."
- "'Structural' interoperability is an intermediate level that defines the structure or format of data exchange (i.e., the message format standards) where there is uniform movement of health data from one system to another such that the clinical or operational purpose and meaning of the data is preserved and unaltered. - "'Semantic' interoperability provides interoperability at the highest level, which is the ability of two or more systems or elements to exchange information and to use the information that has been exchanged. Semantic interoperability takes advantage of both the structuring of the data exchange and the codification of the data including vocabulary so that the receiving information technology systems can interpret the data."
